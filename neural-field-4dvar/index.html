22 · Neural-Field 4D-Var

Strong-constraint 4D-Var (6.1) controls the initial state and reaches every later time by rolling a model forward (1.3). SpaceTimeFieldVar drops the rollout: the whole assimilation window is one continuous field

u(t,s)=fθ(t,s),u(t, s) = f_\theta(t, s),u(t,s)=fθ​(t,s),
(22.1)

where ttt is time, sss the spatial position and fθf_\thetafθ​ a coordinate network whose weights θ\thetaθ are the control, fitted to the observations with the dynamics as a soft constraint (Oh 2025). The analysis is a function, not a grid: it can be evaluated at any (t,s)(t, s)(t,s) and differentiated there.

22.1 Running example: SSH from along-track altimetry¶

Satellite altimeters sample sea-surface height (SSH) along narrow ground tracks, a few thousand scattered (tk,xk,yk,ηk)(t_k, x_k, y_k, \eta_k)(tk​,xk​,yk​,ηk​) points per day with errors σk\sigma_kσk​, and nothing in between. A gridded 4D-Var first interpolates those points onto the model grid (GridPointObs) and then needs a background covariance BBB to spread their information. A neural field reads the tracks as they are (PointObsBatch), and the quasi-geostrophic equations, imposed at collocation points, carry the information along the flow instead of BBB.

The first term is the point-observation cost (11.1) with the field fθf_\thetafθ​ as the observation operator. There is no background term. What plays the role of BBB is the network’s spectral bias: gradient descent from a random initialisation fits smooth, low-frequency functions first (Rahaman et al. 2019), and the weight decay keeps θ\thetaθ near the origin. The PDE term is the physics-informed loss of Raissi et al. (2019); its weight λ\lambdaλ trades fidelity to the equations against fidelity to the data.

22.3 Usage¶

Coordinates put time first, (t,x,y)(t, x, y)(t,x,y), for the field, the observations and the collocation points alike (the convention of the residuals in vardax.examples):

A small SIREN is fitted to 64 noisy samples of the decaying Taylor-Green vortex ψ=sin⁡xsin⁡ye−2νt\psi = \sin x \sin y e^{-2 \nu t}ψ=sinxsinye−2νt with ν=0.1\nu = 0.1ν=0.1, which solves the unforced vorticity equation exactly; the example runs on a CPU in seconds. A real analysis uses a wider and deeper network, more collocation points and a step budget in the tens of thousands.

It prints 300 steps, ‖∇J‖ = 141 and relative error 0.14, cycle state (25,); the large gradient norm is the step budget running out, not convergence.

result.grad_norm reports ∥∇J(θ⋆)∥\lVert\nabla J(\theta^\star)\rVert∥∇J(θ⋆)∥: a first-order optimiser stopped by its step budget is not at a stationary point, which matters if you differentiate through the analysis.

A separable field (one small network per axis, combined by a low-rank product, as in Oh 2025) evaluates a whole space-time grid in one pass and is much faster for dense collocation; any single-example eqx.Module of (t,s)(t, s)(t,s) works as field.

Collocation points¶

A ResidualPenalty with a sampler draws its points once per analysis, from key, and holds them fixed while the minimiser runs. The minimiser assumes a deterministic cost: its patience test and best-iterate tracking compare costs across steps, and the implicit adjoint differentiates the stationarity condition ∇J(θ⋆)=0\nabla J(\theta^\star) = 0∇J(θ⋆)=0. Resampling every step (a stochastic gradient of the expected residual) would break all three, so it is left to a custom training loop; draw enough points to resolve the residual, or refit with fresh keys.

In a cycle, as_analysis_step(grid, key=key) draws the points once, at construction, and every cycle reuses them. pipekit_cycle.DACycle passes no time, so each cycle’s PointObsBatch must use window-relative times in the same window as the collocation points; the step refuses observation times (and a t0t_0t0​) outside the collocation time range.

Choosing λ\lambdaλ¶

The observation term is a sum over NobsN_\text{obs}Nobs​ points weighted by 1/σ21/\sigma^21/σ2, the residual term a mean over the collocation points, so λ\lambdaλ trades off against roughly Nobs/(2σ2)N_\text{obs}/(2\sigma^2)Nobs​/(2σ2) and has to grow with it. The 1-D advection test uses λ=103\lambda = 10^3λ=103 for 100 observations with σ=0.05\sigma = 0.05σ=0.05 (Nobs/(2σ2)=2×104N_\text{obs}/(2\sigma^2) = 2\times10^4Nobs​/(2σ2)=2×104).

Uncertainty: a deep ensemble¶

analysis.ensemble(obs, field_init, n_members, key=key) refits from n_members random initialisations (field_init: key -> field), each with its own collocation draw, under eqx.filter_vmap, and returns one result whose arrays carry a leading member axis. The spread of the members is the deep-ensemble heuristic of Lakshminarayanan et al. (2017): small where observations pin the field down, large away from them (tests/test_spacetime_field.py checks both). Use the neural field when observations are scattered in space and time, when no tangent-linear/adjoint model is at hand but the equations are known, or when the window is long enough that a sequential rollout dominates the cost. Prefer strong 4D-Var when the model is trusted (the residual weight then has to grow without bound to match it) and when a forecast from the analysis must be consistent with the model step by step. The two meet in the strong-constraint variant: fit only u(t0,⋅)u(t_0, \cdot)u(t0​,⋅) as a field and roll it out, StrongFourDVar(..., parameterization=NeuralFieldParameterization(field, coords, alpha)) (chapter 6).
