Observation Operators

Chapter 2 introduced the observation model (2.1), its cost (2.2) and the ObservationOperator protocol, and it defines each operator family. This chapter is the catalogue of the operators vardax ships (masked identity, linear projection, averaging kernel, multi-instrument fusion and scattered point observations) and of their implementation details.

The first four satisfy pipekit_cycle.ObservationOperator (the first three directly; multi-instrument fusion via its .to_observation_operator() adapter, see chapter 2), which tests/test_pipekit_protocols.py checks. These four expose __call__(x) and linearize(x); the adjoint test at the end of this chapter checks the tangent-linear contract.

The Python blocks on this page run in order, each continuing the ones above it.

Masked identity¶

The masked identity (2.3) observes the state x∈RDx\vect{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_x}x∈RDx​ where the mask m∈{0,1}Dy\vect{m} \in \{0, 1\}^{D_y}m∈{0,1}Dy​ has mi=1m_i = 1mi​=1, with Dy=DxD_y = D_xDy​=Dx​. The PRNG keys for the whole page are split here.

It prints IdentityLinearOperator True: the tangent is the identity, so masked residuals come from the cost, which multiplies by m\vect{m}m.

The mask can be soft (mi∈[0,1]m_i \in [0, 1]mi​∈[0,1]) for fuzzy observations. The operator still works, but the obs-error covariance interpretation changes (a soft mask implicitly inflates Σε\gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilonΣε​ where mim_imi​ is small).

Linear projection¶

The linear projection (2.4) applies a stored matrix H∈RDy×Dx\mat{H} \in \mathbb{R}^{D_y \times D_x}H∈RDy​×Dx​. The block builds it from a footprint in which each of Dy=3D_y = 3Dy​=3 receptors averages two neighbouring cells of the Dx=6D_x = 6Dx​=6.

It prints (3,) True.

Use cases:

  • Lagrangian footprint: a particle simulator produces a footprint matrix mapping the source field to receptor observations. The matrix is often sparse (only cells with non-zero footprint contribute), and lineax has no sparse operator, so wrap the sparse product in a lineax.FunctionLinearOperator.

  • Spectral filtering: observe specific frequency bands. Store H=F−1MF\mat{H} = \mathfrak{F}^{-1} \mat{M} \mathfrak{F}H=F−1MF, with F\mathfrak{F}F the Fourier transform and M\mat{M}M the diagonal mask of the retained bands, as a composition of lineax operators.

  • Spatial interpolation: bilinear or bicubic projection from the grid to observation points. The interpolation matrix is sparse.

Averaging kernel¶

The averaging kernel (2.5) is the right model for RTM-derived L2 satellite products; chapter 2 defines it and its symbols, and this section covers its construction and structure. For column CH₄ the weighting w\vect{w}w tends to 1 in the lower troposphere, where the signal comes from the atmosphere, and to 0 in the stratosphere, where the retrieval prior μret\gvect{\mu}_\text{ret}μret​ takes over. The tangent-linear map H=Jx^diag(w)\mat{H} = \mat{J}_{\hat{x}} \op{diag}(\vect{w})H=Jx^​diag(w) inherits the structure of Jx^\mat{J}_{\hat{x}}Jx^​ (sparse, Kronecker, low-rank).

L2 products typically include the averaging-kernel matrix, prior, and weighting as sidecar metadata in the netCDF / HDF5 file.

Structured kernels¶

The state has Nlev=2N_\text{lev} = 2Nlev​=2 levels and Ncol=3N_\text{col} = 3Ncol​=3 columns, so Dx=NlevNcol=6D_x = N_\text{lev} N_\text{col} = 6Dx​=Nlev​Ncol​=6.

Why skipping the AK is wrong¶

Chapter 2 explains why treating an L2 retrieval as y=x+ε\vect{y} = \vect{x} + \gvect{\varepsilon}y=x+ε instead of (2.5) biases the posterior when μret≠μx\gvect{\mu}_\text{ret} \ne \gvect{\mu}_xμret​=μx​. The retrieval folds in information from μret\gvect{\mu}_\text{ret}μret​ that we then attribute to the data, so the MAP is pulled toward μret\gvect{\mu}_\text{ret}μret​.

The bias is largest when:

  • Information content is low (rank-deficient Jx^\mat{J}_{\hat{x}}Jx^​, large 1−w\mathbf{1} - \vect{w}1−w fraction)

  • μret\gvect{\mu}_\text{ret}μret​ differs from μx\gvect{\mu}_xμx​ (e.g. inventory prior vs. climatology)

  • Long inversion windows compound the bias

The fix is to always use AveragingKernel. The cost is one extra mat-vec per observation, which is negligible next to the forward-model cost.

Multi-instrument fusion¶

For multi-satellite inversions, each instrument gets an InstrumentSpec with its own operator, mask mi\vect{m}_imi​ and error covariance Σε,i\gmat{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon,i}Σε,i​. The toy below has a coarse TROPOMI-like instrument behind an averaging kernel and a fine EMIT-like one on the grid, on Dx=6D_x = 6Dx​=6 cells. MultiInstrumentFusion returns the per-instrument predictions, and its cost sums the terms of the fusion cost (2.6), here with αi=1\alpha_i = 1αi​=1.

It prints {'TROPOMI': (3,), 'EMIT': (6,)} and observation cost at the truth 2.57.

Because the fusion happens at the likelihood level (2.6), nothing is regridded to a common grid. Cross-instrument errors default to independent; correlated errors need a block-non-diagonal Σε\gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilonΣε​. The quality mask mi\vect{m}_imi​ lives in each InstrumentSpec, since Batch* has no per-pixel instrument index.

Cross-instrument bias¶

Real instruments disagree systematically. TROPOMI and GHGSat differ by about 5 to 20 ppb on methane retrievals due to spectral calibration, retrieval algorithm differences, and footprint effects. Pretending they agree biases the joint analysis.

For joint inversion with bias estimation (Epic 9), introduce a per-instrument bias state bib_ibi​, which shifts each prediction to hi(x)+bih_i(\vect{x}) + b_ihi​(x)+bi​ and adds a Gaussian prior cost on bib_ibi​, with prior standard deviation σb,i\sigma_{b,i}σb,i​:

It prints bias cost = 0.125.

The bias bib_ibi​ becomes a joint state variable, and the analysis solves for (x,b)(\vect{x}, \vect{b})(x,b) together, with b∈RNinst\vect{b} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_\text{inst}}b∈RNinst​, one bias per instrument. The bias prior tames the underdeterminacy. Hierarchical priors per (instrument, basin, season) are the operational pattern.

This is not yet shipped in vardax; the design is documented for Epic 9.

Scattered point observations¶

Along-track altimetry, drifters and station networks deliver observations at scattered coordinates and times, not on the state grid. Scattering them into a mostly-NaN grid would discard their positions and force one Σε\gmat{\Sigma}_\varepsilonΣε​ for all times. Padding rows contribute exactly zero value and zero gradient. point_obs_cost masks the whitened residual with jnp.where before squaring, and first replaces the padding’s coordinates, values and σi\sigma_iσi​ with finite placeholders. Two operators evaluate h(x)h(\vect{x})h(x) at the points:

  • GridPointObs(grid_axes): for a gridded state. The state has a leading time axis, either a 4D-Var trajectory (x0,…,xT)(\vect{x}_0, \ldots, \vect{x}_T)(x0​,…,xT​) or a 3D-Var state of shape (T+1,Dx)(T + 1, D_x)(T+1,Dx​). Observation iii reads xτi\vect{x}_{\tau_i}xτi​​ and interpolates it linearly (bilinearly on a 2-D grid) at si\vect{s}_isi​, clamped at the grid edge. This is InterpObs’s order=1 stencil, computed at call time so that the coordinates may be traced.

  • FieldPointObs(): for a state that is a callable field x(s)x(\vect{s})x(s), such as a neural field. It returns y^i=x(si)\hat{y}_i = x(\vect{s}_i)y^​i​=x(si​) and passes all DsD_sDs​ columns to the field, a time column included.

ThreeDVar, StrongFourDVar, WeakFourDVar, ReducedBasisFourDVar and IncrementalFourDVar choose their path from the batch type. A PointObsBatch needs obs_op=GridPointObs(...), and the per-observation σi\sigma_iσi​ replaces obs_cov_op, which must be None (or an identity operator; anything else raises ValueError rather than being silently ignored). 4D-Var models compare observation iii with the trajectory state at step τi\tau_iτi​, so hth_tht​ and Σε,t\gmat{\Sigma}_{\varepsilon,t}Σε,t​ can change from step to step. ThreeDVar also accepts FieldPointObs together with a NeuralFieldParameterization, which evaluates the field at the observation coordinates without a grid. A leading batch axis on the batch’s arrays is optional.
