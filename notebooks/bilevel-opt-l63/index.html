10 — Bilevel Optimisation: Learning the Cost Weights

Ported from the mfourdvar Bi-Level Estimation chapter and the argmin differentiation notes.

Two levels¶

A variational analysis x∗(θ)x^*(\theta)x∗(θ) is itself a function of the hyper-parameters θ\thetaθ of its cost — here the weights of the observation and prior terms. Bilevel optimisation treats the inner minimisation as a differentiable layer and tunes θ\thetaθ with an outer objective evaluated on ground truth:

θ∗=arg⁡min⁡θ  L(x∗(θ)),L(x)=∥x−xtrue∥2x∗(θ)=arg⁡min⁡x  U(x;θ),U(x;θ)=αobs∥m⊙(x−y)∥2+αprior∥x−φ(x)∥2.\begin{aligned} \theta^* &= \underset{\theta}{\arg\min}\; L\big(x^*(\theta)\big), \qquad L(x) = \|x - x_{\text{true}}\|^2 \\ x^*(\theta) &= \underset{x}{\arg\min}\; U(x; \theta), \qquad U(x; \theta) = \alpha_{obs} \|m \odot (x - y)\|^2 + \alpha_{prior} \|x - \varphi(x)\|^2 . \end{aligned}θ∗x∗(θ)​=θargmin​L(x∗(θ)),L(x)=∥x−xtrue​∥2=xargmin​U(x;θ),U(x;θ)=αobs​∥m⊙(x−y)∥2+αprior​∥x−φ(x)∥2.​
(1)

The inner problem is the weak-constraint state estimate of notebook 08: yyy are the masked, noisy observations and φ\varphiφ is a learned prior whose reconstruction error plays the role of the model-error term. The outer problem asks a different question of the same cost: not “which xxx minimises UUU?” but “which UUU has a minimiser closest to the truth?”.

Why the weights matter¶

For a linear prior φ(x)=Ax\varphi(x) = Axφ(x)=Ax the inner problem is a quadratic and its minimiser is explicit,

x∗(θ)=(αobsM+αprior(I−A)⊤(I−A))−1αobsMy,M=diag(m),x^*(\theta) = \big(\alpha_{obs} M + \alpha_{prior} (I - A)^\top (I - A)\big)^{-1} \alpha_{obs} M y , \qquad M = \mathrm{diag}(m),x∗(θ)=(αobs​M+αprior​(I−A)⊤(I−A))−1αobs​My,M=diag(m),
(2)

a generalised Tikhonov (Wiener) filter whose regularisation strength is the ratio αprior/αobs\alpha_{prior} / \alpha_{obs}αprior​/αobs​. Too small and the analysis interpolates the noise; too large and it collapses onto the prior’s fixed-point set ker⁡(I−A)\ker(I - A)ker(I−A) — the states the prior reconstructs exactly, which for a trained autoencoder is its learned manifold — and ignores the data. In the Bayesian reading of chapter 1 the weights are inverse error variances and the right ratio is σobs2/σprior2\sigma_{obs}^2 / \sigma_{prior}^2σobs2​/σprior2​; when the prior is a neural network nobody knows σprior\sigma_{prior}σprior​, so the ratio is a knob to be tuned — by hand, by cross-validation, or, as here, by gradient descent on held-out truth.

Differentiating through the argmin¶

The outer gradient is a chain rule through the inner solution,

dLdθ=∂L∂x∣x∗⋅dx∗dθ,\frac{dL}{d\theta} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial x}\Big|_{x^*} \cdot \frac{d x^*}{d\theta} ,dθdL​=∂x∂L​∣∣​x∗​⋅dθdx∗​,
(3)

and everything hinges on the hypergradient dx∗/dθdx^*/d\thetadx∗/dθ. Two routes:

  • Unrolling. Compute xKx_KxK​ by KKK explicit gradient steps xk+1=xk−η ∇xU(xk;θ)x_{k+1} = x_k - \eta\, \nabla_x U(x_k; \theta)xk+1​=xk​−η∇x​U(xk​;θ) and back-propagate through the recursion. Differentiating the update gives the linear recursion dxk+1dθ=(I−η ∇x2U)dxkdθ−η ∂θ∇xU\frac{dx_{k+1}}{d\theta} = \big(I - \eta\, \nabla^2_x U\big)\frac{dx_k}{d\theta} - \eta\, \partial_\theta \nabla_x Udθdxk+1​​=(I−η∇x2​U)dθdxk​​−η∂θ​∇x​U, which is what jax.grad through a lax.scan evaluates. Memory grows with KKK, but the gradient is exact for the analysis you actually compute, converged or not.

  • Implicit differentiation. At a true minimiser ∇xU(x∗;θ)=0\nabla_x U(x^*; \theta) = 0∇x​U(x∗;θ)=0 for every θ\thetaθ, so differentiating that identity (the implicit function theorem) gives dx∗dθ=−[∇x2U]−1∂θ∇xU\frac{dx^*}{d\theta} = -\big[\nabla_x^2 U\big]^{-1} \partial_\theta \nabla_x Udθdx∗​=−[∇x2​U]−1∂θ​∇x​U. The outer gradient then needs one linear solve with the inner Hessian, by conjugate gradients with Hessian-vector products, and no memory of the iterates. It is exact only if the inner solver converged.

The mfourdvar notes summarise the trade as approximate the solution (unrolling) versus approximate the gradient (implicit). Here the inner loop is short and unrolled, so jax.grad unrolls through it; chapter 12 covers optimistix.ImplicitAdjoint for the other route and OneStepAdjoint for the cheap approximation in between.

The same preprocessing pipeline as notebook 08: simulate, cut into windows, mask every other step, add noise, standardise, and split into a training set (used by the outer objective) and a held-out set (used only for the final comparison).

2. A fixed prior¶

The prior φ\varphiφ is a bilinear autoencoder pre-trained on clean trajectories (as in notebook 07) and then frozen: the only things learned below are the two cost weights. Freezing it is what makes this a hyper-parameter problem with two unknowns rather than the full 4DVarNet training of chapter 9, where θ\thetaθ would also contain every weight of φ\varphiφ and of the learned solver — the same bilevel structure with a much larger outer variable.

prior = BilinAEPrior1D(state_dim=N, latent_dim=8, n_time=T, key=jax.random.PRNGKey(10))
pre_opt = optax.adam(1e-2)
pre_state = pre_opt.init(eqx.filter(prior, eqx.is_array))


@eqx.filter_jit
def pretrain_step(prior, opt_state, x):
    loss, grads = eqx.filter_value_and_grad(lambda p: jnp.mean((x - p(x)) ** 2))(prior)
    updates, opt_state = pre_opt.update(grads, opt_state, prior)
    return eqx.apply_updates(prior, updates), opt_state, loss


for _ in range(300):
    prior, pre_state, pre_loss = pretrain_step(prior, pre_state, batch_train.target)
print(f"prior reconstruction MSE after pre-training: {float(pre_loss):.4f}")

3. The inner solver as a differentiable function¶

The hyper-parameters are stored as logs so the weights stay positive. variational_cost is a mean over all elements, so its gradient is tiny per element; scaling the step by the number of elements gives a per-element step size η\etaη that is easy to reason about (it is gradient descent on the equivalent sum cost).

For the quadratic part of the cost the update contracts the error at the observed points by a factor 1−2ηαobs1 - 2\eta\alpha_{obs}1−2ηαobs​ per step, so with η=0.2\eta = 0.2η=0.2 and αobs=0.5\alpha_{obs} = 0.5αobs​=0.5 twenty steps reduce it by 0.820≈0.010.8^{20} \approx 0.010.820≈0.01: the inner loop is close to, but not at, its minimiser, which is precisely the regime where unrolling is the honest choice of hypergradient.

Note the interplay between the weights and the step. Scaling both α\alphaα’s by a constant ccc leaves arg⁡min⁡U\arg\min UargminU unchanged but multiplies the gradient — and therefore the effective step size — by ccc. With η\etaη fixed, the overall scale of (αobs,αprior)(\alpha_{obs}, \alpha_{prior})(αobs​,αprior​) acts as an inner learning rate and their ratio sets the obs/prior trade-off, so the outer loop is really learning two distinct things: how much to regularise, and how far to move in KKK steps.

N_INNER = 20
ETA = 0.2


def weights(hyper):
    return jnp.exp(hyper["log_alpha_obs"]), jnp.exp(hyper["log_alpha_prior"])


def inner_solve(hyper, batch, prior, n_steps=N_INNER, eta=ETA):
    """K steps of gradient descent on U(x; theta), starting from masked obs."""
    alpha_obs, alpha_prior = weights(hyper)
    n_elem = batch.input.size

    def body(x, _):
        g = variational_cost_grad(x, batch, prior, alpha_obs, alpha_prior)
        x = x - eta * n_elem * g
        return x, variational_cost(x, batch, prior, alpha_obs, alpha_prior)

    x0 = batch.input * batch.mask
    x_star, cost_trace = jax.lax.scan(body, x0, None, length=n_steps)
    return x_star, cost_trace


def outer_loss(hyper, batch, prior):
    x_star, _ = inner_solve(hyper, batch, prior)
    return jnp.mean((x_star - batch.target) ** 2)


hyper_fixed = {"log_alpha_obs": jnp.log(0.5), "log_alpha_prior": jnp.log(0.5)}
mse_fixed_train = float(outer_loss(hyper_fixed, batch_train, prior))
print(f"outer loss with fixed weights (0.5, 0.5): {mse_fixed_train:.4f}")
outer loss with fixed weights (0.5, 0.5): 0.0401

4. Outer loop — learn (αobs,αprior)(\alpha_{obs}, \alpha_{prior})(αobs​,αprior​)¶

jax.grad(outer_loss) differentiates through the unrolled inner solver. The outer optimiser is plain Adam on the two log-weights, and the outer objective is the reconstruction error on the training windows: the held-out windows are touched only in section 5, so the comparison there is a fair test of generalisation of the learned weights.

outer_opt = optax.adam(learning_rate=0.05)


@jax.jit
def outer_step(hyper, opt_state):
    loss, grads = jax.value_and_grad(outer_loss)(hyper, batch_train, prior)
    updates, opt_state = outer_opt.update(grads, opt_state, hyper)
    return optax.apply_updates(hyper, updates), opt_state, loss


hyper = dict(hyper_fixed)
opt_state = outer_opt.init(hyper)
hist_alpha, hist_outer = [weights(hyper)], []
for _ in range(150):
    hyper, opt_state, loss = outer_step(hyper, opt_state)
    hist_alpha.append(weights(hyper))
    hist_outer.append(float(loss))
hist_alpha = jnp.array(hist_alpha)

a_obs, a_prior = weights(hyper)
print(f"learned weights: alpha_obs={float(a_obs):.3f}, alpha_prior={float(a_prior):.3f}")
print(f"outer loss: {mse_fixed_train:.4f} -> {hist_outer[-1]:.4f}")

outer loss: 0.0401 -> 0.0049

The inner cost traces show why: the learned weights change the geometry of UUU so that KKK steps land closer to the truth, even though the cost value itself is not what the outer loop optimises. A lower inner cost is not the goal — the truth does not minimise UUU for any weights, since the prior is imperfect and the observations are noisy — so the outer loop is free to pick weights whose minimiser is wrong in a useful direction. This is the general lesson of bilevel DA: the inner cost is an instrument, and ground truth (or any independent validation signal) is what calibrates it.

Summary¶

  • Any analysis produced by a differentiable inner solver is a function of the cost’s hyper-parameters; an outer objective on ground truth turns hand-tuning of the regularisation ratio into optimisation.

  • The hypergradient dx∗/dθdx^*/d\thetadx∗/dθ can be obtained by unrolling the inner iterations (exact for the computed analysis, O(K)O(K)O(K) memory) or by the implicit function theorem at the minimiser (one Hessian solve, O(1)O(1)O(1) memory, exact only at convergence). Here the inner loop is unrolled gradient descent on variational_cost; for large states use optimistix with ImplicitAdjoint (chapter 12) or the one-step adjoint of notebook 02.

  • The same mechanism learns any other cost hyper-parameter — the inner step size, the prior’s own weights (that is 4DVarNet, chapter 9), the ODE parameters of notebook 09, or the update rule itself (notebook 11).
