09 — Parameter Estimation on Lorenz-63

Ported from the mfourdvar Parameter Estimation chapter and its l63_ode_param_* development notebooks.

So far the tutorials have treated the dynamical model as known and estimated only the state. Here the model itself has unknown parameters θ=(σ,ρ,β)\boldsymbol{\theta} = (\sigma, \rho, \beta)θ=(σ,ρ,β), and we recover them — first on their own, then jointly with the initial state — from noisy, partial observations of a few short trajectories.

From Bayes to a cost function¶

The generative story has three ingredients. A dynamical model whose right-hand side depends on θ\thetaθ, an observation model with a mask mtm_tmt​ and Gaussian noise, and prior beliefs about the unknowns:

Dynamics:u˙=f(u;θ),f(u;θ)=(σ(u2−u1),  u1(ρ−u3)−u2,  u1u2−βu3)Observations:yt=mt⊙(ut+εt),εt∼N(0,σobs2I)Priors:u0∼N(ub,σb2I),θ∼flat.\begin{aligned} \text{Dynamics:} \quad & \dot{u} = f(u; \theta), \qquad f(u; \theta) = \big(\sigma(u_2 - u_1),\; u_1(\rho - u_3) - u_2,\; u_1 u_2 - \beta u_3\big) \\ \text{Observations:} \quad & y_t = m_t \odot (u_t + \varepsilon_t), \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_{obs}^2 I) \\ \text{Priors:} \quad & u_0 \sim \mathcal{N}(u_b, \sigma_b^2 I), \qquad \theta \sim \text{flat}. \end{aligned}Dynamics:Observations:Priors:​u˙=f(u;θ),f(u;θ)=(σ(u2​−u1​),u1​(ρ−u3​)−u2​,u1​u2​−βu3​)yt​=mt​⊙(ut​+εt​),εt​∼N(0,σobs2​I)u0​∼N(ub​,σb2​I),θ∼flat.​
(1)

Because the dynamics are deterministic, the whole trajectory is a function of the initial state and the parameters through the flow map φt(u0;θ)\varphi_t(u_0; \theta)φt​(u0​;θ), the solution of the ODE at time ttt:

φt(u0;θ)=u0+∫0tf(φτ(u0;θ);θ) dτ.\varphi_t(u_0; \theta) = u_0 + \int_0^t f\big(\varphi_\tau(u_0; \theta); \theta\big)\, d\tau .φt​(u0​;θ)=u0​+∫0t​f(φτ​(u0​;θ);θ)dτ.
(2)

Numerically the flow map is an ODE solver, and any solver builds it by composing short steps, φt+Δt=φΔt∘φt\varphi_{t + \Delta t} = \varphi_{\Delta t} \circ \varphi_tφt+Δt​=φΔt​∘φt​, which is what makes the one-step and full-trajectory priors below two views of the same object.

The posterior over the unknowns given BBB observed windows is p(u0,θ∣y)∝p(y∣u0,θ) p(u0) p(θ)p(u_0, \theta \mid y) \propto p(y \mid u_0, \theta)\, p(u_0)\, p(\theta)p(u0​,θ∣y)∝p(y∣u0​,θ)p(u0​)p(θ), and taking the negative log turns the Gaussian factors into squared norms. Up to constants the maximum a posteriori estimate minimises

U(u0,θ)=12σobs2∑b∥m⊙(φ(u0b;θ)−yb)∥2⏟data misfit+12σb2∑b∥u0b−ubb∥2⏟background,U(u_0, \theta) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{2\sigma_{obs}^2} \sum_b \big\| m \odot (\varphi(u_0^b; \theta) - y^b) \big\|^2}_{\text{data misfit}} + \underbrace{\frac{1}{2\sigma_b^2} \sum_b \| u_0^b - u_b^b \|^2}_{\text{background}} ,U(u0​,θ)=data misfit2σobs2​1​b∑​∥∥​m⊙(φ(u0b​;θ)−yb)∥∥​2​​+background2σb2​1​b∑​∥u0b​−ubb​∥2​​,
(3)

which is the strong-constraint 4DVar cost of chapter 6 with the control variable extended from u0u_0u0​ to (u0,θ)(u_0, \theta)(u0​,θ) — the augmented state trick of the data-assimilation literature. In code it is strong_variational_cost with a forward_fn that closes over θ\thetaθ. In this sum form the weights αobs=1/2σobs2\alpha_{obs} = 1 / 2\sigma_{obs}^2αobs​=1/2σobs2​ and αbg=1/2σb2\alpha_{bg} = 1 / 2\sigma_b^2αbg​=1/2σb2​ are the inverse error variances, and only their ratio matters for the minimiser. One caveat for reading the code: the library’s costs are means over their elements (the misfit over B⋅T⋅NB \cdot T \cdot NB⋅T⋅N entries, the background over B⋅NB \cdot NB⋅N), so the alpha arguments are algorithmic weights whose variance interpretation carries a factor of TTT — section 5 does the bookkeeping.

What the gradient is¶

Everything below is gradient descent on UUU, so the object that does the work is the sensitivity of the trajectory to the parameters. By the chain rule,

∇θU=∑t(∂φt∂θ) ⁣⊤∂ℓt∂φt,ℓt=12σobs2∥mt⊙(φt−yt)∥2,\nabla_\theta U = \sum_{t} \Big(\frac{\partial \varphi_t}{\partial \theta}\Big)^{\!\top} \frac{\partial \ell_t}{\partial \varphi_t}, \qquad \ell_t = \frac{1}{2\sigma_{obs}^2}\|m_t \odot (\varphi_t - y_t)\|^2 ,∇θ​U=t∑​(∂θ∂φt​​)⊤∂φt​∂ℓt​​,ℓt​=2σobs2​1​∥mt​⊙(φt​−yt​)∥2,
(4)

and the Jacobians ∂φt/∂θ\partial \varphi_t / \partial \theta∂φt​/∂θ obey their own ODE (the tangent-linear model). diffrax never forms them: the RecursiveCheckpointAdjoint used by DynTrajectory propagates the residuals backwards through the solve with the adjoint model, at a memory cost logarithmic in the number of steps. Chapter 12 compares this with the continuous adjoint (BacksolveAdjoint) for long windows; with only three parameters forward-mode sensitivities would be equally cheap here.

The ingredients are the ODE priors from chapter 19: a DynTrajectory wraps a diffrax solve around f(t,u;θ)f(t, u; \theta)f(t,u;θ), threads θ\thetaθ through as the solver args, and lets gradients flow through the integration. In the taxonomy of the mfourdvar notes this is the dynamical model end of the spectrum (F=FdynF = F_{\text{dyn}}F=Fdyn​ with a handful of physical parameters); replacing part of fff by a neural network gives the hybrid models discussed at the end.

import functools as ft

import jax
import jax.numpy as jnp

from vardax import Batch1D, DynIncrements, DynTrajectory, strong_variational_cost

1. The ODE prior with learnable parameters¶

The right-hand side reads θ\thetaθ from the diffrax args slot. Anything passed as params= to the prior (or stored as its default params) lands there, so the same object serves fixed-physics and learnable-physics use. The library’s own Lorenz63 module stores its coefficients as Python floats, which Equinox’s array filter treats as static; for estimation we want them as an array so that jax.grad sees them.

Lorenz-63 is the standard testbed because it is the smallest system with the property that makes parameter estimation in geophysics hard: chaos. Its largest Lyapunov exponent is λ1≈0.9\lambda_1 \approx 0.9λ1​≈0.9, so two trajectories separate by a factor eee every ≈1.1\approx 1.1≈1.1 time units. The windows below are 0.4 time units long — short enough that the cost in (u0,θ)(u_0, \theta)(u0​,θ) is smooth and unimodal, long enough to constrain the parameters. Chapter 6 discusses what happens to the strong-constraint cost as the window grows past the Lyapunov time.

THETA_TRUE = jnp.array([10.0, 28.0, 8.0 / 3.0])


def lorenz63_rhs(t, u, theta):
    sigma, rho, beta = theta
    x_, y_, z_ = u
    return jnp.array([sigma * (y_ - x_), x_ * (rho - z_) - y_, x_ * y_ - beta * z_])


# Defaults: Tsit5, adaptive PID controller, RecursiveCheckpointAdjoint.
prior = DynTrajectory(model=lorenz63_rhs, params=THETA_TRUE)

2. Generate observed windows¶

We integrate BBB short windows from initial conditions drawn off the attractor and observe every other step with Gaussian noise. Gaps are stored as NaN — the convention of real geophysical products — so the cost is evaluated with nan_to_num=True (the mask removes those entries from the misfit, but 0⋅NaN0 \cdot \text{NaN}0⋅NaN would still poison the gradient).

The off-attractor start matters, and the reason is visible in the equations. The only place σ\sigmaσ enters is the term σ(u2−u1)\sigma (u_2 - u_1)σ(u2​−u1​), so the sensitivity of the trajectory to σ\sigmaσ is driven by ∫(u2−u1) dτ\int (u_2 - u_1)\, d\tau∫(u2​−u1​)dτ. On the attractor the first two components track each other closely most of the time, that integral stays small, and σ\sigmaσ is nearly invisible from short windows: the cost is flat in σ\sigmaσ to within the noise floor. Starting away from the attractor produces a transient during which u1≠u2u_1 \neq u_2u1​=u2​, and that transient is what carries the information. Section 4 makes this quantitative.

DT = 0.01

3. Stage 1 — known initial state, unknown θ\thetaθ¶

With u0u_0u0​ fixed to the truth the only control is θ\thetaθ. The strong-constraint cost has no background term (alpha_bg=0), and forward_fn is the batched rollout with θ\thetaθ bound:

θ∗=arg⁡min⁡θ  ∑b∥m⊙(φ(u0b;θ)−yb)∥2.\theta^* = \underset{\theta}{\arg\min}\; \sum_b \big\| m \odot (\varphi(u_0^b; \theta) - y^b) \big\|^2 .θ∗=θargmin​b∑​∥∥​m⊙(φ(u0b​;θ)−yb)∥∥​2.
(5)

This is nonlinear least squares. We use Adam rather than a Gauss–Newton or L-BFGS solver because it needs nothing but the gradient, its per-coordinate normalisation copes with ρ≈28\rho \approx 28ρ≈28 and β≈2.7\beta \approx 2.7β≈2.7 living on different scales, and the notebook is about the gradient rather than the optimiser; optimistix.LevenbergMarquardt on the residual vector would converge in far fewer iterations.

At the solution the cost cannot fall below the noise floor: with the true parameters the misfit is just the noise, so U(θtrue)≈12σobs2⋅σobs2⋅#{observed}U(\theta_{\text{true}}) \approx \frac{1}{2\sigma_{obs}^2} \cdot \sigma_{obs}^2 \cdot \#\{\text{observed}\}U(θtrue​)≈2σobs2​1​⋅σobs2​⋅#{observed}, which in the normalised units printed below is σobs2×\sigma_{obs}^2 \timesσobs2​× (observed fraction) =0.5= 0.5=0.5. A fit that lands below the floor is fitting noise; a fit far above it has not converged or is stuck.

def forward_fn(x0, ts, theta):
    return jax.vmap(lambda x0_i: prior(x0_i, ts, params=theta))(x0)


def cost_theta(theta, x0, ts, batch):
    return strong_variational_cost(
        x0,
        ts,
        batch,
        forward_fn=ft.partial(forward_fn, theta=theta),
        alpha_obs=1.0,
        alpha_bg=0.0,
        nan_to_num=True,
    )


theta_init = jnp.array([8.0, 24.0, 2.0])
opt = optax.adam(learning_rate=0.1)


@jax.jit
def step_theta(theta, opt_state):
    loss, grads = jax.value_and_grad(cost_theta)(theta, x0_true, ts, batch)
    updates, opt_state = opt.update(grads, opt_state, theta)
    return optax.apply_updates(theta, updates), opt_state, loss

4. Identifiability — the cost along each parameter axis¶

Sweeping one parameter at a time (others held at the truth) shows how sharply the data constrain each of them. Around its minimiser θ^\hat\thetaθ^ the realised cost is approximately quadratic,

U(θ)≈U(θ^)+12(θ−θ^)⊤∇2U(θ^) (θ−θ^),U(\theta) \approx U(\hat\theta) + \tfrac{1}{2}(\theta - \hat\theta)^\top \nabla^2 U(\hat\theta)\, (\theta - \hat\theta),U(θ)≈U(θ^)+21​(θ−θ^)⊤∇2U(θ^)(θ−θ^),
(6)

and for a least-squares cost the Hessian splits into a Gauss–Newton part built from first derivatives plus terms weighted by the residuals,

∇2U=1σobs2∑b,tJb,t⊤ diag(mt) Jb,t⏟I(θ)+1σobs2∑b,t∑i(mt⊙rb,t)i ∇θ2(φt)i,Jb,t=∂φt(u0b;θ)∂θ.\nabla^2 U = \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sigma_{obs}^2} \sum_{b,t} J_{b,t}^\top\, \mathrm{diag}(m_t)\, J_{b,t}}_{\mathcal{I}(\theta)} + \frac{1}{\sigma_{obs}^2}\sum_{b,t} \sum_i \big(m_t \odot r_{b,t}\big)_i\, \nabla^2_\theta (\varphi_t)_i , \qquad J_{b,t} = \frac{\partial \varphi_t(u_0^b; \theta)}{\partial \theta}.∇2U=I(θ)σobs2​1​b,t∑​Jb,t⊤​diag(mt​)Jb,t​​​+σobs2​1​b,t∑​i∑​(mt​⊙rb,t​)i​∇θ2​(φt​)i​,Jb,t​=∂θ∂φt​(u0b​;θ)​.
(7)

The first part is the Fisher information I\mathcal{I}I of the experiment (the expected curvature; the residual-weighted part averages to zero under the noise model). Its inverse is the Cramér–Rao bound on the covariance of any unbiased estimator, so the curvature of each profile is not a property of the optimiser but of the data: a flat direction means the observations cannot tell those parameter values apart, and no amount of iteration will fix it. Two honest caveats about the plots: for one noisy realisation the minimiser θ^\hat\thetaθ^ sits near, not exactly at, the truth (the score ∇U(θtrue)\nabla U(\theta_{\text{true}})∇U(θtrue​) is small but not zero), and each profile is a one-dimensional slice — parameter correlations need the full matrix, which chapter 13 builds with GaussNewtonHessian.

The horizontal line is the noise floor σobs2⋅\sigma_{obs}^2 \cdotσobs2​⋅ (observed fraction) =0.5= 0.5=0.5. Try re-running section 2 with windows cut from a long on-attractor trajectory: the σ\sigmaσ curve flattens to within a few percent of the floor, and gradient descent wanders — which is exactly what happened in the first draft of this notebook.

5. Stage 2 — joint estimation of u0u_0u0​ and θ\thetaθ¶

In practice the initial state is not known either. The control becomes the PyTree (u0,θ)(u_0, \theta)(u0​,θ); the background ubu_bub​ is the noisy observation at t=0t = 0t=0, and the background term keeps u0u_0u0​ anchored to it while θ\thetaθ is far from the truth. Everything else is unchanged — the cost function is indifferent to what x0 and params are made of.

Two things are worth noticing about the joint problem.

  • It is the same problem as 4DVar. Chapter 6 minimises over u0u_0u0​ with θ\thetaθ fixed; notebook 01 does exactly that with StrongFourDVar. Adding θ\thetaθ to the control vector costs three more coordinates and nothing else, which is why “augmented state” is the standard DA route to parameter estimation.

  • The weights encode relative trust — after normalisation. strong_variational_cost averages the misfit over B⋅T⋅NB \cdot T \cdot NB⋅T⋅N entries and the background over B⋅NB \cdot NB⋅N, so per element the observation weight is αobs/T\alpha_{obs} / Tαobs​/T relative to αbg\alpha_{bg}αbg​. Matching to the Gaussian sum cost gives σb2/σobs2=αobs/(αbgT)=1/(0.1⋅40)=0.25\sigma_b^2 / \sigma_{obs}^2 = \alpha_{obs} / (\alpha_{bg} T) = 1 / (0.1 \cdot 40) = 0.25σb2​/σobs2​=αobs​/(αbg​T)=1/(0.1⋅40)=0.25: with these settings the background counts as twice as precise as a single observation. The true background error here is one observation’s worth of noise, so this over-trusts ubu_bub​ by a factor of four in variance — and the fit still moves u0u_0u0​ a long way, because sixty observed values per window outweigh three background values. The exact Gaussian weighting would be αbg=αobs/T\alpha_{bg} = \alpha_{obs} / Tαbg​=αobs​/T; try it, and then let the next notebook learn such weights instead of reasoning them out by hand.

The analysed trajectory — the rollout of the estimated u0u_0u0​ with the estimated θ\thetaθ — fills the gaps and denoises the observations. Because the dynamics are a hard constraint, the analysis is by construction a solution of the ODE: the observations can only choose which solution, through u0u_0u0​ and θ\thetaθ, never bend it. That is what the strong constraint buys (smooth, physically consistent fields between observations) and what it costs (no way to absorb model error).

6. Weak-constraint variant — one-step increments¶

When a densely sampled trajectory is available, the DynIncrements prior offers a cheaper route. Its loss is the sum of one-step residuals,

R(u;θ)=∑t∥ut+1−φΔt(ut;θ)∥2,R(u; \theta) = \sum_t \big\| u_{t+1} - \varphi_{\Delta t}(u_t; \theta) \big\|^2 ,R(u;θ)=t∑​∥∥​ut+1​−φΔt​(ut​;θ)∥∥​2,
(8)

so every step is an independent short solve (vectorised with vmap) and no long rollout — or initial-state estimate — is needed. This is the model-error residual of weak-constraint 4DVar (chapter 7) with the states held fixed: each term asks how far the observed state at t+1t+1t+1 is from where the model would have put it, and θ\thetaθ is chosen to make those increments as small as possible. The gradient only ever passes through one step of the solver, so this cost is smooth even for windows far longer than the Lyapunov time.

The price is that noise in the trajectory enters the residual on both sides. Writing ut=uˉt+εtu_t = \bar u_t + \varepsilon_tut​=uˉt​+εt​ for the true state plus noise and linearising the flow map,

ut+1−φΔt(ut;θ)≈(uˉt+1−φΔt(uˉt;θ))+εt+1−∂φΔt∂u εt,u_{t+1} - \varphi_{\Delta t}(u_t; \theta) \approx \big(\bar u_{t+1} - \varphi_{\Delta t}(\bar u_t; \theta)\big) + \varepsilon_{t+1} - \frac{\partial \varphi_{\Delta t}}{\partial u}\,\varepsilon_t ,ut+1​−φΔt​(ut​;θ)≈(uˉt+1​−φΔt​(uˉt​;θ))+εt+1​−∂u∂φΔt​​εt​,
(9)

and the last term depends on θ\thetaθ through the Jacobian. Least squares with noise in the regressors is the errors-in-variables problem: the estimator is exact on clean data and increasingly biased as the noise grows, in contrast with the strong-constraint fit where noise sits only in the target and averages out. Fitting θ\thetaθ to dense trajectories at increasing noise levels shows this directly, and it is why the strong-constraint formulation above is preferred for sparse or noisy data.

inc_prior = DynIncrements(model=lorenz63_rhs)


def cost_increments(theta, x_traj, ts):
    return jax.vmap(lambda x_b: inc_prior.loss(x_b, ts, params=theta))(x_traj).sum()


opt_inc = optax.adam(learning_rate=0.1)


@jax.jit

    updates, opt_state = opt_inc.update(grads, opt_state, theta)
    return optax.apply_updates(theta, updates), opt_state, loss

Summary¶

  • Parameter estimation is 4DVar with an augmented control vector: the MAP estimate of (u0,θ)(u_0, \theta)(u0​,θ) minimises the same strong-constraint cost, with the weights set by the error variances.

  • DynTrajectory + strong_variational_cost turn it into ordinary gradient descent: θ\thetaθ rides along as the diffrax args, and jax.grad differentiates through the ODE solve with a checkpointed adjoint.

  • Identifiability is a property of the data, not the optimiser. The curvature of the cost at the truth is the Fisher information; sweep it before trusting a fit, and design the experiment (here: off-attractor transients) so the flat directions disappear.

  • DynIncrements gives the one-step (weak-constraint) residual for fitting θ\thetaθ to dense trajectories — cheap and smooth, but an errors-in-variables estimator that noise biases.
